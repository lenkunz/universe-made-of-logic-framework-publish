Title: **Why Does Ketchup Refuse to Flow Until You Hit It?**  
Subtitle: *The bottle is not broken. The sauce changes its response while you force it.*

## Central mystery

Turn a ketchup bottle upside down and the sauce can remain stubbornly in place. Shake, squeeze, or tap it and suddenly it moves. Water does not behave this way: pushing water harder does not make water acquire a radically lower viscosity. So what changed in the ketchup?

The tempting answer is “the hit knocked it loose.” That is not wrong as kitchen advice, but it hides the more interesting physics. Ketchup belongs to the world of complex fluids, where the relation between applied shear and flow is not captured by one constant viscosity.

## Ordinary external explanation / observations

### 1. Newtonian viscosity is the simple case

For an ideal Newtonian fluid at fixed temperature and pressure, shear stress is proportional to shear rate. The proportionality constant is viscosity. Water is a familiar approximation: changing how quickly you shear it does not normally reorganize it into a dramatically different flowing state.

Ketchup is different. Measurements of commercial and formulated ketchups repeatedly find non-Newtonian, pseudoplastic or **shear-thinning** behavior: apparent viscosity decreases as shear rate increases. Kumbár et al. measured commercial and homemade tomato ketchup and fitted the flow with a power-law model, with flow indices below one. Koocheki et al. likewise found pseudoplastic behavior across tested hydrocolloid concentrations and temperatures.

### 2. Ketchup is structured material, not a simple molecular liquid

Ketchup contains water plus tomato solids and, depending on formulation, starches and hydrocolloids. Studies describe ketchup as a suspension and weak gel with viscoelastic behavior. Its resistance to flow therefore depends on an internal network and particle organization, not merely on molecules sliding past one another as in a simple low-molecular-weight liquid.

In starch-thickened samples, microscopy and rheology show swollen/disrupted starch granules and tomato-pulp particles. At higher shear rates, particles can orient along flow and deform, contributing to lower apparent viscosity. Different thickeners and concentrations measurably change the flow curve, yield stress and apparent viscosity.

This matters because there is no single universal “ketchup viscosity.” The number depends on formulation, temperature, shear history and the shear conditions under which it is measured.

### 3. Yield stress and shear thinning are related kitchen experiences but not identical ideas

Many ketchup descriptions and rheological fits include a **yield stress** or a tendency toward one: below some stress the material can behave approximately solid-like over the relevant observation, while above it sustained flow becomes much easier. Separately, **shear thinning** means that once it is shearing, its apparent viscosity decreases as shear rate increases.

Real ketchup can also show thixotropic/time-dependent effects, depending on formulation and test. These distinctions should not be collapsed into the slogan “hit it and viscosity disappears.”

A useful model for many yield-stress/shear-thinning materials is the Herschel–Bulkley form

`tau = tau_y + K * gamma_dot^n`

with `n < 1` for shear thinning. Not every ketchup dataset is best described by exactly the same model, and some studies find a simple power law fits better. The episode should use the equation only as optional grounding, not as the narrative center.

### 4. The bottle trick is a change in interaction, not a magical switch in substance

NIST uses ketchup as an accessible example of a non-Newtonian material: an upside-down bottle may not pour, but a sharp tap can make the ketchup move much more readily. The force changes the flow conditions and temporarily changes how easily the structured material moves.

This is why squeezing a flexible bottle, shaking it, or tapping it can work. Each supplies deformation/shear that the resting sauce did not have. The exact result also depends on bottle geometry, trapped air, wall slip and formulation, so “tap exactly here” is not a universal law of ketchup.

## What remains conceptually interesting

The physics already answers why ketchup flows. The conceptual surprise is that a familiar word like “thickness” sounds as though it names a fixed property carried by the sauce in isolation.

But for a complex fluid, the observed resistance to flow belongs to a **state plus interaction**. Ask how thick the ketchup is without saying how it is being sheared and you have left out part of the question.

The sauce in the bottle before and during the squeeze is chemically still ketchup. Yet the same material can participate in very different mechanical responses because its internal organization and current forcing differ.

That makes ketchup a compact lesson in a broader habit of thought: nouns encourage us to package behavior inside objects, while experiments often reveal behavior at the meeting between structure and circumstance.

## Examples and research notes

**Kitchen contrast:** honey is viscous but much closer to a Newtonian liquid over ordinary conditions. Tapping does not produce the same dramatic shear-thinning response. The comparison is useful, but avoid claiming honey is perfectly Newtonian under all possible conditions.

**Paint:** many paints are engineered to be shear-thinning so they spread under brushing/rolling yet resist running once applied. This broadens the lesson beyond condiments.

**Ceramic 3D printing:** NIST notes that printable ceramic pastes are also non-Newtonian. Their flow under extrusion and ability to hold shape afterward make rheology technologically important.

**Do not use cornstarch/oobleck as if it were the same mechanism.** Oobleck is famous for shear thickening, the opposite direction of response. It can be mentioned briefly as evidence that “non-Newtonian” names a family, not one behavior.
