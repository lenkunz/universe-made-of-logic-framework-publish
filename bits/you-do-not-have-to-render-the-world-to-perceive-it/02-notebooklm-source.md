# You Do Not Have to Render the World to Perceive It

*Seeing is not the same job as drawing every pixel.*

Slug: `you-do-not-have-to-render-the-world-to-perceive-it`
Status: External-science-led perception episode with an explicitly exploratory framework extension. Source revision: **R20260911-RHY-01**.

## Central mystery

A graphics processor can be asked to produce a frame containing millions of pixel values. The display needs a definite color and brightness at each location it will show.

Looking at the display feels rich and continuous.

That feeling tempts us to imagine a second display inside the head: photons enter the eyes, the brain redraws the whole scene, and consciousness watches the internal picture.

But who watches that picture?

And why would perception need to do the same job as the screen?

The central question is:

> **Does seeing a detailed world require consciousness to maintain a uniformly detailed inner rendering of it?**

The useful answer is not “the brain does nothing.” Neural and bodily activity are indispensable to ordinary scientific accounts of vision.

The answer is that **perceiving and producing a raster image are different tasks**. Perception can be selective, task-directed, active, and supported by repeated access to the world.

## 1. A screen must output the pixels it shows

A rendered frame is an output surface.

If a display is to show a blue patch at one location and a red edge at another, the graphics pipeline must eventually provide enough information for those displayed distinctions.

This says nothing mysterious about graphics processors. It only clarifies their task.

The processor may use many efficiencies—reuse, caching, level of detail, compressed assets, temporal methods, or selective rendering—but the final display still has to emit the image that appears on its surface.

Human perception is not required to produce that same surface for another observer inside the skull.

The GPU comparison should therefore be framed as:

`produce an externally inspectable image`

versus:

`resolve enough structure to guide recognition, attention, memory, and action`.

It is not a fair watt-for-watt or operation-for-operation benchmark.

## 2. Change blindness punctures the intuition of a complete inner screen

Rensink, O'Regan, and Clark presented alternating versions of natural scenes with a brief blank between them. Under those conditions, observers could take surprisingly long to detect a substantial change when the interruption prevented the usual local motion signal from drawing attention to the changed location.

The result is called change blindness.

The important point is not that observers are literally blind to the scene. They can see, recognize objects, and eventually identify the change.

The point is that the subjective richness of vision does not guarantee that every detail is held in a complete, stable, instantly comparable inner copy.

Attention matters to which difference becomes reportable.

This constrains the inner-screen intuition, but it does not settle every theory of visual representation. A careful episode must not turn “people miss changes” into “the brain represents nothing.”

## 3. Inattentional blindness shows that visibility and report are not the same

Simons and Chabris asked participants to watch people passing basketballs and count passes made by one team. During the task, an unexpected event crossed the scene. Many participants failed to report it.

The experiment is memorable because the event can look obvious once attention is directed toward it.

Again, the lesson is not that the event was physically absent or that the eyes received no light from it.

The lesson is that:

`available in the visual field`

does not guarantee:

`resolved into the task-relevant, reportable experience`.

The current task changes what receives attention and what becomes consequential to the observer.

That gives the framework a legitimate ordinary doorway without making the experiment evidence for the framework itself.

## 4. Eyes and hands can be part of the working arrangement

Ballard, Hayhoe, Pook, and Rao describe natural tasks through **deictic codes**: temporary bindings that connect external sensory data with cognitive programs and motor actions. At the time scale they study, eye, head, hand, and body orientation can help organize what is needed next.

This offers a different picture from building one exhaustive scene model and consulting it for every step.

A person can look back at the object.

The hand can point, orient, or hold a place.

The environment can keep information stable enough to be sampled again.

The body's current arrangement can serve as part of working memory for the task.

None of this means there are no internal representations. The paper proposes a computational level where embodied orientation and external structure matter.

The broad ordinary lesson is:

> **Some cognition can be cheaper because the task reuses the world and the body instead of reconstructing every relation internally at once.**

“Cheaper” here is ordinary computational language, not yet framework budget.

## 5. The world as outside memory is a theory, not a settled verdict

O'Regan and Noë's sensorimotor account treats seeing as skilled activity involving regularities between movement and sensory change. On that account, the world can function like an “outside memory” available through active exploration, rather than vision requiring a complete inner replica.

This comparison is unusually close to the question of this Bit.

It must still be presented with its status intact.

The sensorimotor account is a theory of vision and visual consciousness. It has generated extensive argument and commentary. Change blindness alone does not prove it, and this Bit should not pretend that cognitive science has concluded that visual representation is unnecessary.

The earned ordinary-language statement is narrower:

> **Rich visual experience does not by itself demonstrate a pixel-complete inner display. Perception depends on selective attention, active sampling, task structure, memory, and bodily relation.**

## 6. The framework asks a different question from neuroscience

The framework uses **perception** more broadly than conscious seeing.

Perception is the domain-relative resolution of an encounter:

`relation + domain + available support`

→ `resolved relation on Stage`.

A human visual experience is one rich case. An instrument, atom, body, or other domain can also resolve a relation at its own grain without human awareness.

The framework therefore does not begin by asking how a completed world is copied into an observer.

It asks:

> **Which distinctions does this encounter make consequential, and at what grain can they resolve?**

That fits the ordinary perception evidence in one structural way. The person can encounter a rich scene while only some differences become consequential to the present task.

Coarse does not mean blurry. “Players passing a ball” can be a sharp, useful, action-guiding relation while an unexpected event remains unresolved in report.

## 7. Why the whole world does not need to be rendered twice

The framework source says an encounter need not expose its whole supporting network.

What appears on one Stage can be the coarse consequence of many still-active relations. The receiving domain does not need an explicit internal description of every supporting relation for the current distinction to resolve.

Applied to visual perception, the proposal becomes:

`world relation`

→ `body/sensory encounter`

→ `enough distinction resolves for the current task`

→ `attention or action changes the next encounter`.

This avoids the need for a tiny observer watching a complete internal screen.

It also preserves a role for the body. Eye movements, posture, hands, and action are not merely commands issued after perception finishes. They can help determine which relation is encountered next.

## 8. The stronger brain/body proposal

Len's stronger proposal goes beyond the ordinary scientific points above.

It is not merely:

> “The brain skips some pixels,”

or:

> “Consciousness does not calculate a tongue or hand trajectory step by step.”

The proposed ontology is:

> **The perceptual domain projects; relation and Stage resolve the supported continuation; neural, bodily, and motor activity are the coupled physical expression or interface of that resolution rather than a separate ontological origin that first computes it.**

This proposal must be represented at full strength and full uncertainty.

Changing the brain, nerves, body, or environment can change perception, projection, and action. The “interface” is not inert and is not optional. Neural activity may precede visible movement. Disrupting a supporting signal can interrupt the continuation.

The proposal has not yet produced an experimental discriminator from conventional neural control accounts.

So this is not established neuroscience. It is an unresolved framework mechanism that motivates a different causal interpretation of the same coupled events.

## 9. Heat is not resolved parameters

The original discussion noticed that graphics hardware can consume substantial power and produce heat while drawing continuous images, then asked whether the brain avoids that cost because consciousness does not render an internal movie.

The comparison is useful only if its boundary remains visible.

Physical heat in a processor comes from ordinary electrical and material processes. Framework budget is not heat, watts, joules, transistor switching, or neural metabolism.

Likewise, a brain and a GPU solve different problems with different architectures. Their energy use or operation counts cannot be compared as though both were trying to produce the same output.

The earned conceptual contrast is about **required distinctions**, not a derived thermodynamic law:

> A display pipeline must make the displayed frame available at its output surface. A perceptual system may only need the distinctions that guide the present relation and the next action.

## 10. What this changes about “seeing”

The everyday feeling of vision is a world available around us.

The inner-screen metaphor turns that availability into an imagined object: a complete picture continuously rendered inside the head.

But availability can be relational.

The wall remains there to be looked at again.

The eyes can move.

Attention can shift.

The body can approach.

Memory can preserve a useful constraint.

The next encounter can resolve finer detail when it becomes consequential.

This does not make the unseen wall unreal. It means that perceiving the wall does not require every possible detail of the wall to be equally resolved inside one current act of attention.

## Status and claim boundary

### Established external observations

- change detection can fail under interruptions that prevent a local transient from drawing attention;
- people can fail to report a conspicuous unexpected event while performing a demanding attention task;
- eye, hand, head, and body orientation can participate in organizing natural tasks.

### External theoretical comparison

- deictic/embodied and sensorimotor accounts provide models in which active relation with body and environment matters;
- they do not establish this framework or prove that the brain has no representations.

### Framework-native ground

- perception is domain-relative Stage resolution;
- coarse does not mean blurry or unreal;
- an encounter need not expose its whole supporting network;
- fine resolution is required where the present relation makes a distinction consequential.

### Exploratory framework proposal

- neural/body activity may be the coupled Stage expression/interface of perceptual-domain resolution rather than its separate ontological origin.

### Not established

- the brain performs no computation in ordinary scientific terms;
- consciousness works without neural and bodily support;
- change blindness proves the world is unrendered;
- the brain is a literal giant quantum computer;
- heat is framework-resolved parameters;
- framework budget can be measured in watts or FLOPS.

## Ending

Return to the display.

The screen must show its pixels because showing pixels is its job.

Perception has another job: keep a living relation coherent enough to notice, recognize, choose, and act.

> **Seeing the world does not have to mean drawing the whole world again inside you.**
