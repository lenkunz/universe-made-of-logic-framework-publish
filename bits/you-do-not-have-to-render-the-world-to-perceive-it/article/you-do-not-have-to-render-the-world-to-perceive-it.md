# You Do Not Have to Render the World to Perceive It

*Seeing is not the same job as drawing every pixel.*

![A monitor displays a dense color mosaic while a person turns toward and reaches for a real cup in a richly furnished room.](you-do-not-have-to-render-the-world-to-perceive-it.webp)

> **Image Caption:** A display must emit the pixels it shows. Perception can return to the world and resolve the detail the present task makes consequential.

A computer draws a room on a screen. You sit beside the screen, looking around a room that is already there.

It is tempting to imagine that the two jobs are basically the same. The graphics system makes an image out there; somewhere behind your eyes, the brain must make another image in here. Perhaps it has a much better renderer. Perhaps it draws an astonishing number of details without letting you notice the work.

But what requires the second picture?

The display has to produce the visible colors and brightness of its image. Your task might be to find the cup, read a sentence, or avoid the chair. Those are different output requirements. A useful comparison with a GPU begins there, at the job being done.

It does not begin with a contest over electricity or computing power. I am not assigning equivalent workloads to a brain and a graphics card, or claiming that either calculates every pixel from scratch. The question is whether perceiving a scene requires a uniformly detailed inner display of that scene.

The feeling that the room is richly present does not settle how that presence is achieved.

Consider the flicker experiments reported by Ronald Rensink, J. Kevin O'Regan, and James Clark in 1997. Observers saw an original scene alternate with a modified version, with brief blank fields between them. Even substantial, repeated changes could become difficult to identify. A verbal cue helped people find the change much faster. Objects judged important within a scene also attracted faster detection. [Rensink, O'Regan, and Clark's paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1997.tb00427.x)

The blank matters: the tested arrangement removes the usual localized motion cue that would draw attention to the change. The result concerns detection under those conditions. It does not establish that observers had no representation of the scene, or that unattended objects ceased to exist. It challenges the easy assumption that looking at a scene gives us ready access to every difference between successive versions.

A different experiment keeps an unexpected event in view while giving the observer another job.

In Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris's 1999 study, participants watched people pass basketballs and counted passes by an assigned team. An unexpected person crossed the action, wearing a gorilla costume or carrying an umbrella. Many participants failed to report the event. Detection varied with the task and the display conditions, including how demanding the counting was and how the unexpected event related visually to the attended team. [Simons and Chabris's paper](https://www.chabris.com/Simons1999.pdf)

This is inattentional blindness. The unexpected event was physically present in the video. Being available to the eyes did not guarantee that it became something the observer could report. The finding does not say people generally see nothing outside their task. It shows that visible presence and successful noticing can come apart in a demanding situation.

So far, these are failures of detection. What does successful activity look like?

Dana Ballard, Mary Hayhoe, Polly Pook, and Rajesh Rao examined that question through their account of *deictic codes*: using eye, hand, and body orientation to bind a task to particular things in the environment.

In their block-copying task, participants reproduced a pattern of colored blocks. They often looked back at the model more than once while copying a single block. A look could supply its color; another could supply where to place it. The authors interpreted such sequences as gathering the needed information close to the step that used it, reducing what had to be held at once. [Ballard and colleagues' 1997 paper](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~dana/bbs.pdf)

This was a particular task and a proposed account of its organization, not a complete explanation of vision. Still, it gives a positive alternative to the full-picture assumption: a person can use temporary, task-specific references and revisit the world as the activity unfolds.

O'Regan and Alva Noë developed a more ambitious theoretical alternative in their 2001 sensorimotor account. They proposed that seeing depends on skilled engagement with the ways sensory input changes as an organism acts. The world can serve as an external resource available for exploration, rather than requiring a complete inner duplicate to generate experience. [O'Regan and Noë's paper](https://whatfeelingislike.net/wp-content/plugins/zotpress/lib/request/request.dl.php?api_user_id=195193&content_type=application%2Fpdf&dlkey=EYDV25L7)

That is a theory of perception and visual consciousness, not a consensus established by the earlier experiments. Its claims go beyond showing that attention affects detection. The useful comparison here is its emphasis on continuing access: richness may depend partly on what an organism can do and consult, not only on what is simultaneously represented in an internal picture.

We can now state a provisional ordinary answer.

Visual experience does not, by itself, prove a uniformly detailed inner raster. The studies give reasons to take selection, active sampling, and the demands of a task seriously. The sensorimotor theory offers one interpretation of how those features might contribute to seeing. Neither the experiments nor that interpretation licenses the claim that the brain contains no visual representations.

And the tiny inner screen is a tempting metaphor, not a fair description of every conventional neural account. A scientific explanation can already include selective processing, memory, eye movements, and bodily interaction. Recognizing those features does not require adopting my framework.

The distinction I want to carry forward is simpler:

> **Having a world available to encounter is not the same as having every detail displayed at once.**

Imagine reaching for the cup beside the monitor. The practical question is what must be settled for this reach to succeed. The cup has to be accessible in a usable way. The supporting relation includes a body, a surface, a handle, and the conditions under which the hand can meet it.

There is no requirement in that description to also settle the lettering on every book spine in the room. Yet those books remain capable of entering another encounter. If you turn to read one, a different set of distinctions becomes consequential.

That is an illustration of the question, not another experimental result. It also has a boundary: returning to the world does not guarantee that the world has waited unchanged. The cup can move. The next encounter must meet what is actually supported then.

The framework gives this a particular language.

A *Stage* is where a relation meets an anchoring domain and resolves in a way that can matter there. Perception is domain-relative in that sense: what becomes available belongs to an encounter at a particular grain, with particular support.

This does not make a conscious observer the creator of the room. Nor does it turn the Stage into a screen located inside a head. It names the meeting through which a relation becomes locally consequential.

At that grain, a coarse relation can be exact about the distinctions it carries. **Coarse** does not mean blurry, weak, or inaccurate. A distinction can be sufficiently resolved to guide the present encounter without being a complete account of the participating things.

The framework also does not make the supporting network disappear. An encounter can expose a usable result while the relations within its participants continue doing their own work. A coarse outward comparison is not permission to treat everything inside it as inactive.

That separation between a relation and everything supporting it is the ground developed in the earlier perception Bit.

There is a stronger proposal here, and I want to state it without hiding its strength.

Perhaps the perceptual domain projects, the supported continuation resolves on Stage, and neural, bodily, and motor activity is the coupled physical expression or interface of that resolution. On this proposal, the brain is not a separate ontological origin that first calculates a complete internal world and then hands its result to an otherwise waiting perceiver.

This is more than saying the brain skips some pixels. It proposes a different account of what is originating and what is being physically expressed.

It remains exploratory. None of the four sources above derives that ontology. Their findings and arguments must retain their own scientific and theoretical meanings instead of becoming evidence for a stronger claim they never tested.

Calling bodily activity an expression also leaves it inside the consequential relation. The brain, nerves, muscles, and environment remain real participants. Changing their support can change or interrupt what becomes possible. The proposal does not describe a completed action elsewhere followed by an optional neural after-image.

The decisive missing step is an experimental discriminator: **what measurable outcome would this account predict differently from a conventional neural account?** Selective attention and repeated looking are already available to those accounts. Calling their activity Stage resolution does not by itself create a new prediction. The framework has not yet supplied the differing observation and the conditions under which it should occur.

That limit lets us keep the useful conceptual question without presenting it as completed neuroscience.

It also keeps the GPU comparison honest. Physical heat, energy use, operations per second, and neural metabolism are not interchangeable with the framework's relational budget. A task-level contrast supplies no conversion between them. Calling the world available does not turn the environment into a literal quantum computer performing a hidden rendering job on our behalf.

The modest claim and the stronger proposal can therefore stand at different levels. There are empirical reasons to question effortless access to a fully detailed scene. There are theories of active perception that address that problem. The framework adds its own account of consequential resolution, then an exploratory interpretation of the body's role. These steps do not inherit equal evidential status merely because they share a direction.

Return to the monitor and the cup.

The monitor must show its image. To pick up the cup, you have to encounter the cup. Whether the second activity requires another fully drawn world inside you is the assumption we were examining, not something the richness of the room had already proved.

> **Seeing the world does not have to mean drawing the whole world again inside you.**
