# A Prediction Does Not Have to Be a Number

![An amber marker rests beside a laboratory sample box as a researcher begins lifting its lid; a second box remains closed](./a-prediction-does-not-have-to-be-a-number.webp)

> **Image Caption:** A prediction takes a position before the result is opened.

Suppose I tell you before opening a box:

> The object inside should be heavier than the one on the table.

I have not told you its mass.

But I have still taken a risk.

If the object comes out lighter, my claim has a problem. If it has the same mass, that does not satisfy “heavier” either.

That small example exposes something easy to forget when we talk about prediction. A prediction does not become a prediction only when it contains six decimal places. What matters first is whether it commits to a result **before the result is known**.

Numbers can make that commitment wonderfully sharp. Predict 12.4 within a stated margin, and nature has little room to be polite.

But direction can commit too:

- more rather than less;
- positive rather than negative;
- here rather than there;
- present when X is present, absent when X is removed.

Those claims leave many numerical details open, but they do not leave every outcome open.

That is the important boundary.

A statement like “something interesting should happen” is not much of a prediction. Almost any result can be folded back into it afterward.

A useful structural prediction needs at least three pieces:

1. **The premise:** under what conditions does the claim apply?
2. **The expected direction or structure:** what should those conditions produce?
3. **The result that counts against it:** what opposite or missing outcome would give the claim a problem?

The third piece is the teeth.

We also have to settle what counts as “more,” “here,” or “absent” before looking. A claim loses its risk if those meanings move with the result.

This matters for My GUT Deduction because the framework currently develops structural consequences more readily than measured numbers. Its status document describes its strongest prospective claim as structural rather than a promise to calculate every concrete future state.

That gives us a way to state a claim early. It also gives us a responsibility to keep the claim fixed.

Take the halo-overlap proposal. Imagine two galaxy-cluster halos overlapping. Within the framework, the premise is that their overlap requires additional shared relation to become supported. The proposed physical consequence is a **positive overlap-associated excess beyond ordinary additive contributions**.

In ordinary words: account for what the two halos would contribute anyway, then ask whether an extra contribution remains associated with their overlap.

That example is still a **Candidate correspondence**. Its amplitude and detectability have not been derived. The existence of the proposed physical excess has not been established.

A positive-looking bridge is therefore not automatically confirmation. Ordinary additive halo contributions, baryonic structure—the ordinary matter involved—projection, reconstruction effects, and other conventional contributions all have to be accounted for before a residual can count toward this proposed test.

The absence of an amplitude does not erase the direction. A reliably negative residual under the stated conditions would count against the proposed positive excess.

But “we detected nothing” needs more care. Without an expected minimum effect, a positive contribution could still lie below the analysis's sensitivity. A null result can count against a version that committed beforehand to a detectable excess. Otherwise, it can constrain the allowed size without ruling out every positive value.

That limit must be admitted before the analysis, not invented afterward to rescue the claim.

Likewise, a positive residual that repeatedly survives the stated controls would count in the proposal's favor without uniquely confirming its explanation.

The prediction can become stronger later. A derived scaling law would narrow the target. A numerical amplitude would narrow it again and help decide which observation could detect it.

Prediction is not a switch that flips from “nothing” to “equation.”

It can gain resolution.

The rule I want to keep is simpler:

> **A direction stated before the observation is a prediction when the opposite direction was genuinely allowed to count against it.**

A number is one of the sharpest ways to make that bargain with reality.

It is not the only way to sign the contract.
