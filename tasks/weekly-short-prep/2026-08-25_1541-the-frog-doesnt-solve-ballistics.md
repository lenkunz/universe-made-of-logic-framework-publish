# The Frog Doesn't Solve Ballistics

*The expensive part can happen before the movement does.*

A frog sees a fly.

Then its tongue goes exactly where the fly needs to be caught.

It is tempting to describe this as a tiny physics problem happening inside the frog: estimate distance, estimate motion, calculate a trajectory, send the answer to the tongue, execute.

But that picture quietly assumes something strange. It assumes that every successful action has to be worked out from scratch at the moment it happens.

Most skilled action does not look like that.

Think about catching a falling cup. Reaching for a light switch in a familiar room. Typing a password your fingers know better than your conscious attention does. A pianist landing on the next chord. A bird adjusting its wings in a gust.

The final movement can be fast because much of the work that makes it possible happened earlier.

The body has history.

Its structure has history.

Its nervous system has history.

Its repeated encounters with the world have already removed enormous numbers of bad possibilities.

By the time action becomes necessary, the problem is no longer:

> Out of every physically possible movement, which one should happen?

It is closer to:

> Given everything already constrained, what is still left to resolve?

That is a much cheaper question.

## The path can carry the work

Imagine walking through an unfamiliar building for the first time.

Every junction costs attention. Which corridor? Which staircase? Which door?

Walk the same route every day and something changes. The building did not become smaller. Your destination did not move closer. But the route stops presenting itself as a fresh tree of possibilities.

History has turned many decisions into path.

This does not mean nothing is happening during the walk. You still respond to a blocked hallway, another person, a wet floor, a door that is unexpectedly locked. New distinctions still have to be made when they matter.

What disappears is the need to reopen every distinction that has already been settled well enough for the present task.

That gives a useful way to think about fast action:

**speed does not always come from computing faster. Sometimes it comes from having less left to compute.**

The frog is useful because the movement looks difficult when described from outside. We can write equations for projectile motion. We can measure angles and distances. We can reconstruct the path afterward.

But the existence of a mathematical description does not mean the animal has to reproduce that description internally before it moves.

A map of an action is not necessarily the mechanism that produced the action.

## Constraint first, detail later

This is where the idea connects to My GUT Deduction.

In the framework, a present state is not just a collection of objects waiting for the next calculation. Seated relations become part of the ground available to later relations. Repeated structure can become reusable structure. A path can therefore change what becomes cheap to resolve next.

The framework currently states this simply:

> **Path is memory.**

It also makes a second distinction that matters here: a coarse constraint does not need to contain every fine detail of its eventual implementation.

You can know what an action must accomplish without first specifying every microscopic event by which it will happen.

That does not make the fine events unreal. It means they do not all need to be independently chosen in advance.

A large amount of possibility can remain unresolved until the environment, the body, and the already-seated path make a particular distinction consequential.

So the picture becomes:

`history + present constraint + available structure`

→ `many possibilities already excluded`

→ `small remainder becomes consequential`

→ `action resolves through the path`

The framework's claim is not that frogs violate mechanics, or that biology needs no physical implementation. The physical movement still has to occur, and the framework does not yet provide a quantitative biological model of frog predation.

The claim is narrower and more structural:

**a successful outcome does not require every detail of that outcome to be freshly computed before execution.**

That matters far beyond frogs.

Practice can work this way. Habit can work this way. A well-designed tool can work this way. A stable institution can work this way. Even a physical path can carry constraints that make some later continuations easier than others.

What looks from the outside like a complicated real-time calculation may be the visible end of a much longer history of constraint.

The frog does not need to solve the whole space of ballistics at the moment its tongue leaves its mouth.

By then, most of the problem may already be gone.
