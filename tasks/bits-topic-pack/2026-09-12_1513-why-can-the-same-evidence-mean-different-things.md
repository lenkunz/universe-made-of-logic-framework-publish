## A. Topic/content source

# Why Can the Same Evidence Mean Different Things?

*An observation can stay fixed while the explanations around it move.*

Imagine a medical test comes back positive. The result is real, yet “How likely is the patient to have the disease?” cannot be answered from the positive result alone. We need to know how often the test is positive when disease is present, how often it is positive when disease is absent, and how common the disease was before testing.

That separation is the heart of Bayes’ theorem:

`P(H|E) = P(E|H) P(H) / P(E)`

`P(H)` is the prior, `P(E|H)` the likelihood, and `P(H|E)` the posterior. Confusing `P(E|H)` with `P(H|E)` is a classic mistake. For comparing two explanations, a likelihood ratio asks whether the observation was more expected under one hypothesis than another.

So “my explanation can explain the evidence” is weak by itself. A better question is: **would this evidence also have been easy to get if my explanation were wrong?**

Two people can therefore accept the same datum and disagree about its evidential force because they use different background assumptions, compare different hypotheses, or disagree about how expected the observation is under each. That does not make all interpretations equally good. Evidence discriminates when competing hypotheses make meaningfully different expectations.

Chronology matters too. A specific expectation recorded before an observation constrains interpretation differently from a flexible story assembled afterward. Surprise is useful only comparatively, and interpretive freedom can make post-hoc matches cheap.

My GUT Deduction’s current evidence methodology enters here. Its canonical statement is:

> **Evidence is observation becoming constraint on the available interpretations.**

It records a qualitative profile `E_O(x) = <C,S,I,R,F^-1>`: chronology constraint, specificity, independence, recurrence, and interpretive freedom. This is deliberately not a scalar probability score. It is a vocabulary for preserving why observations may constrain an ontology differently without pretending uncertainty has already been numerically solved.

The guardrail is essential: correspondence can be evidence relevant to an ontology without proving a particular causal story inside it. A match does not by itself establish intention, a sender, a hidden mechanism, or a tutorial.

The framework profile is **not Bayes’ theorem**. It does not calculate posterior probabilities, likelihoods, or model selection. Bayesian inference is established quantitative probability theory; the framework profile is qualitative ontology-level bookkeeping. Their connection here is narrower: neither treats a datum as though one explanation were printed on its surface.

The closing question is: **When two people see the same fact and reach different conclusions, are they really disagreeing about the fact, or about the world in which that fact would be surprising?**
