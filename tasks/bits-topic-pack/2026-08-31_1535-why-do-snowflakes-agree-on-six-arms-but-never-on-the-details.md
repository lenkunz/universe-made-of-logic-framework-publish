# Why Do Snowflakes Agree on Six Arms but Never on the Details?

### A snow crystal can obey a strict shared rule without carrying a blueprint for every branch.

A familiar stellar snowflake looks almost contradictory. Its arms agree on six directions with striking order, yet the detailed branching differs from crystal to crystal. The useful question is not simply why snowflakes are symmetrical. It is how a strong shared constraint can coexist with enormous fine variation.

Ordinary ice supplies the first part of the answer. NOAA/NESDIS explains that snow crystals grow as water vapor deposits onto ice, and the molecular structure of ordinary hexagonal ice is reflected in the familiar sixfold symmetry. That does not require every crystal to be a flat six-armed star. Plates, columns, needles, dendrites and other forms can all grow within the crystallographic constraint. Atmospheric ice also has exceptions: stacking-disordered ice can produce threefold-looking forms, so “all snowflakes are perfect sixfold stars” is a simplification.

Growth conditions supply the second part. The classic snow-crystal morphology diagram shows strong dependence on temperature and supersaturation. Around roughly -2 °C plate-like forms are common; around -5 °C columns and needles; near -15 °C thin plates and stellar dendrites can grow under suitable supersaturation. Different crystal facets incorporate molecules at different rates, and those rates depend on temperature and surrounding conditions. Water vapor must also diffuse through air to reach the surface.

Technical crystal-growth work describes this as diffusion-limited growth with anisotropic surface energy and anisotropic attachment kinetics. Small protrusions can gain better access to vapor and grow faster, amplifying perturbations into dendritic branches. The lattice biases the directions; diffusion, kinetics, instability, and the current shape determine how those directions fill in.

The six arms can resemble one another because they share the same crystal structure and, at a given moment, broadly the same surrounding temperature and humidity. They do not need to communicate a detailed construction plan. Real asymmetries still arise from local fluctuations, defects, collisions, riming, orientation and other disturbances.

Two crystals then diverge because they do not travel through exactly the same sequence of atmospheric conditions. Growth is cumulative: the shape produced under one condition becomes the starting geometry for the next. A snowflake is therefore a physical history growing inside constraints.

This makes a deeper distinction visible: **shared rule is not shared detail**. The sixfold structure can be robust while branches vary. Temperature can constrain a family of likely morphologies without selecting one exact crystal. History can narrow what comes next without containing the final picture from the beginning.

Only here does My GUT Deduction enter. Its current canonical source defines coarse as a real constraint that leaves some finer distinctions unseated because the present relation does not require them, and stresses that coarse can permit several precise futures rather than one fuzzy future. Snowflakes provide a useful analogy: the broad crystallographic constraint is not blurry or weak, yet it does not specify every branch.

The analogy must stop there. Snowflakes do not prove the framework, and framework `resolution` or `budget` must not replace crystallography, thermodynamics, diffusion, attachment kinetics, or instability as the scientific explanation.

The conceptual payoff is:

> **A rule can be exact about the structure it constrains without specifying every detail that later grows inside that structure.**

The closing question is:

> **If a rule can remain exact while leaving countless details open, how much of the order we see in nature is a blueprint, and how much is constraint becoming specific only as history grows?**
