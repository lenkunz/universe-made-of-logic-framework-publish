## A. Topic/content source

# Why Does a Shadow Have a Blurry Edge?
*The object can have a perfectly sharp edge. The shadow records which parts of a light source each point can still see.*

## Ordinary mystery
Put your hand near a wall under a small bright lamp. Its shadow can look sharply cut. Move the hand away from the wall, leaving the lamp fixed, and the edge often grows soft. Your hand did not become fuzzy. What changed?

## Established optical explanation
A real lamp usually emits light from an **extended area**, not a mathematical point. Imagine a particular spot on the wall asking which points on the lamp's luminous surface are visible around the hand. Outside the shadow it sees the full source. In the darkest **umbra**, it sees none of that source directly. In the **penumbra**, it sees only a fraction. Across the penumbra, that visible fraction changes continuously. Thus the illumination changes gradually even though the hand's boundary is physically sharp.

A point-like source in an ideal ray-optics setup can cast a geometrically sharp boundary. An extended source produces overlapping slightly displaced shadows from its many emitting locations. Larger angular source size generally means a broader penumbra for a given object-screen separation. Increasing the gap between the hand and wall generally broadens the penumbra; bringing the hand closer to the wall narrows it. This is geometry, not a claim that the hand has uncertain edges.

A practical comparison: an LED's tiny emitting area can yield a relatively crisp silhouette; a large diffused window gives a much softer one. Two lights can make two overlapping shadow patterns. Real scenes also involve reflected ambient light, scattering, diffraction, sensor resolution, and visual contrast, so an observed edge is not determined by source size alone.

## The eclipse-scale version
The same geometry appears when the Moon blocks sunlight. NASA distinguishes an umbra, where the Sun is completely obscured, from a penumbra, where the Sun is only partly obscured. An observer inside the penumbra experiences a partial eclipse; inside the umbra, a total eclipse. The Moon has a definite silhouette, yet different locations receive different fractions of the extended solar disk. The shadow therefore has a region of partial illumination. ESA explains how rays from the Sun's edges trace these zones.

## What remains conceptually interesting
The wall does not receive an intrinsic property named “the hand's shadow.” It receives illumination determined jointly by the light source's extent, the hand's position, the wall's position, and the intervening paths. A sharp object can produce a soft-edged result because the result belongs to the entire source–occluder–receiver arrangement.
