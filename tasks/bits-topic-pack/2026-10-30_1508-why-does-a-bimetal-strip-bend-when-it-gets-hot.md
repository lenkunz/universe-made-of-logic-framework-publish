# Why Does a Bimetal Strip Bend When It Gets Hot?

*Each metal only wants a different length. The curve appears because the bond makes them share one shape.*

Heat one free metal strip and it mainly gets longer. Bond it tightly to a different metal that wants to expand by a different amount, and heating can make the pair bend.

Different solids generally have different coefficients of thermal expansion. If two strips lie loose beside each other, heating can simply leave one slightly longer than the other. Neither needs to curve.

A bimetal strip removes that freedom. Its layers are firmly bonded along their length. When heated, the higher-expansion layer wants to lengthen more than the lower-expansion layer, but the interface requires them to remain compatible.

The mismatch becomes internal stress plus curvature. The composite can accommodate some of the difference by placing the higher-expansion material on the outside of the bend, where the path is longer, and the lower-expansion material on the inside, where the path is shorter. NIST/NBS descriptions of thermostat metals explicitly describe this differential-expansion mechanism and the resulting deformation.

> **The curve does not belong to either free strip. It appears because two different preferred expansions have been required to share one geometry.**

That is why bimetal elements are useful in thermometers, thermostats, overload cutouts, and other thermal switches. Temperature change can become mechanical motion without requiring a motor. Coils and spirals can turn distributed curvature into a larger pointer or switching movement.

The amount of bending is not determined by expansion mismatch alone. Layer thicknesses, elastic moduli, geometry, temperature range, bonding quality, and residual stress matter. NBS Circular 486 gives thermostat-metal curvature relations involving expansion-coefficient difference, elastic moduli, thicknesses, and temperature change. NIST thin-film work likewise uses curvature as evidence of coupled thermal and mechanical mismatch in bonded systems.

So the ordinary scientific payoff is:

> **Constraint can convert a difference that would have appeared as two separate lengths into a new response of one composite object.**
