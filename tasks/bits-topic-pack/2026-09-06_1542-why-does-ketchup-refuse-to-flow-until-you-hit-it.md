# Why Does Ketchup Refuse to Flow Until You Hit It?

*The bottle is not broken. The sauce changes its response while you force it.*

Turn a ketchup bottle upside down and the sauce can remain stubbornly in place. Shake, squeeze, or tap it and suddenly it moves. Water does not behave this way in ordinary use: pushing it harder does not make it acquire a dramatically lower apparent viscosity. The tempting kitchen answer is simply that the hit knocked the ketchup loose, but the deeper answer belongs to rheology.

For an ideal Newtonian fluid at fixed conditions, shear stress is proportional to shear rate and viscosity is the proportionality constant. Ketchup is a complex fluid. Measurements of commercial and formulated ketchup repeatedly find non-Newtonian pseudoplastic, or **shear-thinning**, behavior: apparent viscosity decreases as shear rate increases. Kumbár et al. measured commercial and homemade ketchup and fitted their flow with a power-law model with flow indices below one. Koocheki et al. likewise found pseudoplastic behavior across tested hydrocolloid concentrations and temperatures.

Ketchup is also structured material rather than a simple low-molecular-weight liquid. It contains water, tomato solids and, depending on formulation, starches or hydrocolloids. Rheological studies describe ketchup as a suspension and weak gel with viscoelastic behavior. In starch-thickened samples, microscopy shows swollen/disrupted starch granules and tomato-pulp particles. Under increased shear, particles can orient along flow and deform, contributing to reduced apparent viscosity.

Many ketchup formulations also show a **yield stress** or a tendency toward one. Below some stress the material can behave approximately solid-like over the relevant observation; above it sustained flow becomes much easier. This is related to the bottle experience but is not the same definition as shear thinning. Shear thinning describes how apparent viscosity falls as shear rate rises once the material is being sheared. Some formulations also show time-dependent thixotropy. These terms should not be collapsed into one slogan.

The exact rheology depends on formulation, temperature, shear history, solids, thickener and measurement conditions. There is therefore no universal single “ketchup viscosity” that describes every situation. A Herschel–Bulkley model can describe many yield-stress/shear-thinning materials, while some ketchup datasets are fitted well by simpler power-law forms.

NIST uses the upside-down ketchup bottle as an accessible non-Newtonian example: a tap changes the forcing and can make the sauce much easier to move. Similar rheological questions matter in paints and ceramic 3D-printing pastes, where material should flow during application or extrusion but hold shape afterward.

The conceptual puzzle remains after the rheology is understood. Ordinary language makes “thickness” sound like a fixed number stored inside the sauce. But for a structured complex fluid, observed resistance to flow belongs to a **state plus interaction**. Asking how thick ketchup is without saying how it is being sheared leaves out part of the question.
