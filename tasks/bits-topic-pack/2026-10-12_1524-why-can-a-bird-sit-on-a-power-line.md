# Why Can a Bird Sit on a Power Line?

*The wire can be thousands of volts above ground. What matters to the bird is the difference across its body.*

A small bird can perch on an energized power line that would be lethally dangerous for a person to contact under the wrong conditions. The easy story says that the bird is safe because it is not touching the ground. That is useful shorthand, but it hides the deeper electrical idea: current through a body depends on the potential difference established across that body and the impedance of the available path.

The wire may be at a very large potential relative to ground while the bird's two feet, separated by only a short distance along the same good conductor, sit at nearly the same potential. With little voltage difference across the bird, little current is driven through its body.

### Voltage is a difference

OpenStax defines electric potential difference between two points as change in electric potential energy per unit charge. The familiar word *voltage* normally refers to this difference. A quoted electrical potential therefore always needs a reference, even when everyday language leaves the reference implicit.

Source: https://openstax.org/books/college-physics/pages/19-1-electric-potential-energy-potential-difference

Saying that a conductor is at high voltage relative to ground does not mean that the same high voltage exists between two nearby points on that conductor.

### Current needs a driven path

For a simple ohmic path, `I = ΔV/R`. Real bodies and power systems require more detailed impedance and field analysis, but the conceptual point survives: current through the body depends on a potential difference across it and on the electrical properties of the path.

Source: https://openstax.org/books/college-physics/pages/20-2-ohms-law-resistance-and-simple-circuits

A real power-line conductor has finite resistance and carries current, so the bird's two feet are not guaranteed to be at exactly identical potential. Over the small spacing between the feet on a low-resistance conductor, however, the difference is normally too small to drive dangerous body current. The careful phrase is **small potential difference**, not magical perfect equipotentiality.

### Why touching ground changes the situation

Ground matters because power systems commonly use earth or grounded structures as a reference and return path. If a bird simultaneously touches an energized conductor and grounded hardware, its body can bridge a much larger potential difference.

But ground is not the only danger. A bird can also be electrocuted by bridging **two energized parts at sufficiently different potentials**. The U.S. Geological Survey describes bird electrocution occurring when appendages bridge two energized parts or an energized and grounded metal part. Large birds are particularly vulnerable when their bodies or wings can span conductor spacing.

Source: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/electrocution

> **The bird is not safe because electricity 'needs the ground.' It is usually safe because its body does not bridge a large enough potential difference to drive dangerous current through it.**

### The useful counterexample: step potential

The same logic appears during electrical faults on the ground. Two points on the earth can be at different potentials. A person or animal whose feet span those points can experience a dangerous current without touching an overhead wire. OSHA's electrical-safety material explicitly treats hazardous potential differences and equipotential zones.

Source: https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.269AppC

So the ordinary scientific payoff is:

> **Electrical consequence depends on the potential difference established across the participant and the path that relation completes.**
